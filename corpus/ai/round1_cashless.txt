Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of a cashless society

A cashless society, defined as an economy where financial transactions are conducted through digital means instead of physical banknotes and coins, was first approached by Sweden during the 2010s, with the Riksbank reporting that cash payments accounted for only around 10% of all transactions by 2020 (Riksbank, 2021). Meanwhile, the World Bank has recorded that approximately 1.4 billion adults worldwide still remain unbanked, meaning that they do not hold any account for receiving digital payments (Demirgüç-Kunt et al., 2022). These contrasting figures have aroused concerns from certain parties over whether the disappearance of cash would benefit or harm the public, particularly the elderly and low-income groups. This essay, therefore, will examine both the benefits and drawbacks of a cashless society.

The premier advantage of a cashless society lies in the convenience and efficiency of digital payments for both consumers and businesses. Arvidsson (2019) indicated that mobile payment applications, such as Swish in Sweden, have consistently been used for settling debts between friends and purchasing goods in small shops, reducing the time and labour cost of handling cash. He pointed out that retailers no longer need to count banknotes at the end of each day or pay for cash-in-transit services, which allows them to allocate resources to other operations. For instance, in China, the total value of mobile payments reached 355 trillion yuan in 2021, proving that digital transactions have already become part of citizens' daily routines.

Besides the improvement in efficiency, a cashless society can also assist the government in fighting crime and tax evasion. Physical cash, being anonymous and untraceable, has traditionally been abused for money laundering, bribery and the financing of illegal activities. According to the Indian Ministry of Finance (2017), digital transactions leave verifiable records that enable authorities to track suspicious flows of money, thereby increasing the cost of corruption. For example, after the Indian government withdrew high-denomination banknotes in 2016 with the aim of cracking down on the shadow economy, the number of income tax returns filed grew by around 25% in the following year, presumably because more activities were brought into the formal economy.

However, a cashless society, considered a form of digital exclusion, involves inequality issues. Elderly citizens and residents of rural areas, who are less familiar with smartphones and stable internet access, would be punished by the disappearance of cash counters and ATMs. In accordance with a survey conducted by the UK Financial Conduct Authority (2022), around 1.1 million adults in the United Kingdom remained unbanked, most of whom relied entirely on cash to receive wages and pay bills. The severity of exclusion can vary depending on the country's infrastructure, ranging from minor inconvenience to a complete loss of access to essential services, which has led to protests in countries like Nigeria after the central bank restricted cash withdrawals in 2023.

Another drawback of a cashless society is the erosion of privacy and the rising threat of cybercrime. Since every digital transaction records the time, location and counterparties of a purchase, companies and governments are able to build detailed profiles of consumers' behaviour, which may be abused for commercial or surveillance purposes. Research has also shown that the frequency of card fraud and phishing attacks has been increasing along with the growth of digital payments, particularly in countries with rapid adoption of mobile banking. Although banks usually compensate victims of unauthorised transactions, the anxiety created by these crimes still discourages a portion of the public from abandoning cash.

To conclude, a cashless society is not only an effective way to raise the efficiency of payments and strengthen the monitoring of illegal activities, but consumers can also enjoy the convenience brought by mobile applications. Nevertheless, the adoption of digital payments can be unequal in effect, as the elderly and the unbanked would be deprived of their access to financial services. In addition, the possibility of mass data leakage and fraud also creates a deterrent effect for individuals to fully embrace digital money. Despite these concerns, the transition towards a cashless society should still proceed gradually, as the value of efficiency overrides the temporary inconvenience, and governments, after a short period of adaptation, should provide cash alternatives for vulnerable groups.
