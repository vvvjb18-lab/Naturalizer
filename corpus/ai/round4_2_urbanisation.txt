Describe the cause and effect of urbanisation

Cities have become the default home of humanity. Over half of the world's population are already living in urban areas, and the share is forecast to touch 68% by 2050, meaning that another 2.5 billion people will move into cities within three decades (UN-Habitat, 2022). This scale of movement has no precedent in history. It has been driven by the search for employment and by the pressures that push farmers away from the countryside, while its consequences now shape the economy, the environment and the health of billions of residents. Therefore, this essay explores the causes of urbanisation driven by industrial restructuring and rural hardship, discusses how cities concentrate wealth and opportunity, and examines why uncontrolled urban growth overwhelms housing and public services.

Firstly, the concentration of industry and services in cities is a key cause of urbanisation. Young people in rural areas can rarely find employment that matches their education, since factories, companies and universities are overwhelmingly located in urban centres where infrastructure and customers gather. The World Bank (2021) noted that urban income in developing countries is often double or even triple the rural income for the same occupation, which gives village youth a strong push towards the cities. Additionally, the mechanisation of agriculture has eaten steadily into the demand for farm labour. One combine harvester can replace dozens of workers during the harvest season, and the displaced villagers naturally head towards the cities in search of wages.

Secondly, rural hardship, including poverty, conflict and environmental degradation, pushes residents away from the countryside. In countries torn by years of fighting such as Syria and South Sudan, villages and farmland have been destroyed, forcing millions of families to leave their homes, with the majority ending up in urban areas rather than refugee camps (UNHCR, 2022). Meanwhile, soil erosion, drought and river pollution have made traditional farming increasingly unreliable across parts of Africa and South Asia. Therefore, many migrants aspire to settle permanently in cities and give their children access to schools and hospitals that their home regions cannot provide, resulting in a steady increase in urban populations.

On the other hand, urbanisation also brings significant economic gains. Cities concentrate workers, capital and knowledge within a small area, which allows specialisation and cooperation that would be impossible in scattered villages. It is widely estimated that urban regions contribute over 80% of the global GDP despite covering less than 3% of the land surface (UN-Habitat, 2022). Migrants who reach cities usually gain higher wages, better education and wider career choices, and the money they send back home supports the development of their original communities. For instance, remittances sent by overseas workers and urban residents have become a major source of household income in countries like the Philippines, reaching tens of billions of dollars each year (World Bank, 2023).

Nevertheless, uncontrolled urban growth exerts severe pressure on housing and public services. More than one billion people currently live in slums or informal settlements without safe water, sanitation or secure tenure, and the figure keeps rising in fast-growing cities of Africa and Asia (UN-Habitat, 2022). The influx of the newcomers also dilutes medical and educational resources, resulting in overcrowded classrooms, congested roads and hospitals that are permanently understaffed. Air pollution from traffic and factories further weakens residents' lungs over the years, with urban outdoor pollution linked to millions of premature deaths annually (WHO, 2022). The pursuit of a better life thus turns, for the poorest newcomers, into a threat to health itself.

In conclusion, urbanisation is primarily driven by the concentration of jobs in cities and by the hardships of rural life. It concentrates wealth and opportunity, boosting the productivity of entire nations. However, it also places a significant strain on housing, services and the environment. To sum up, while urbanisation improves individual prospects, further planning is necessary to absorb new arrivals without collapsing public services. Possible solutions, such as investing in secondary cities and rural infrastructure, should be offered to spread opportunity more evenly across each country's territory.
