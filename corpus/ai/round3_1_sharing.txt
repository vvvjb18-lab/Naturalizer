Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of the sharing economy

The sharing economy, defined as an economic model in which individuals borrow or rent assets and services owned by others through online platforms, was first popularised by companies such as Airbnb and Uber in the late 2000s, when the global financial crisis pushed consumers to seek cheaper alternatives to hotels and taxis. The sector's global revenue was estimated to grow from around 15 billion dollars in 2014 to 335 billion dollars by 2025 (PwC, 2015), proving the speed of its expansion. Meanwhile, the rise of platform work has aroused concerns from certain parties over whether this model genuinely creates value for the public, or whether it simply transfers risks from companies to individual workers. This essay, therefore, will examine both the benefits and drawbacks of the sharing economy.

The premier advantage of the sharing economy lies in the more efficient use of idle resources and the lower prices offered to consumers. A private car, for example, typically sits parked for over 90% of its lifetime, and platforms such as Uber connect this wasted capacity with passengers who need temporary access to transport. Zervas et al. (2017) indicated that the entry of Airbnb reduced the revenue of hotels in Texas by around 8% to 10%, pushing the traditional industry to lower its prices and improve the country's service standards. He pointed out that competition, once extended to ordinary households, benefits consumers who previously had limited choices. For instance, travellers in Barcelona can now stay in local apartments at half the price of a downtown hotel room during the peak season.

Besides the improvement in efficiency, the sharing economy also creates flexible income opportunities for workers. Hall and Krueger (2018) found that the majority of Uber's driver-partners treated the platform as a supplementary source of earnings instead of a full-time job, since they were free to decide when and how long to work each day. This flexibility particularly suits students, parents with young children and employees who hold another part-time position. Therefore, platform work has consistently been used as a buffer during economic downturns, presumably helping households to survive sudden losses of salary.

However, the sharing economy, considered a form of disguised employment, involves the exploitation of workers. Platform workers often earn below the local minimum wage once fuel, insurance and vehicle depreciation are deducted, and the absence of contracts means that they receive no paid leave, no pension and no protection against unfair dismissal (ILO, 2021). The severity of precarity can vary across platforms. It ranges from moderate income instability among drivers to complete account deactivation without explanation, and the algorithmic management of these platforms constantly monitors workers' behaviour, which has led to complaints about the loss of autonomy and dignity.

Another drawback of the sharing economy is the pressure it places on housing and urban communities. Garcia-López et al. (2020) revealed that the spread of short-term rentals in Barcelona raised residential rents in the city centre, as landlords converted long-term homes into more profitable tourist accommodation, and similar effects have been documented in cities like Lisbon and Amsterdam. Such conversion directly reduces the supply of housing for local residents, worsening the affordability crisis that many European cities were already facing. Tens of thousands of licenses were eventually suspended with the aim of protecting residents' living conditions (Barcelona City Council, 2021), which triggered lengthy legal battles with the platforms and prompted a proposal for minimum standards across the European Union (European Commission, 2022).

To conclude, the sharing economy is not only an effective way to activate idle resources and lower consumer prices, but workers can also enjoy flexible income that traditional employment rarely offers. Nevertheless, the transfer of business risks to individuals can be immoral in effect, as platform workers would be deprived of the basic protections that employees take for granted. In addition, the conversion of homes into tourist flats also creates a deterrent effect for cities to welcome this model without restriction. Despite these concerns, the sharing economy should still be regulated rather than banned, as the value of efficiency overrides the temporary inconvenience, and governments, after a short period of adaptation, should classify platform work under new categories of employment.
