Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of a four-day working week

A four-day working week, by which employees complete their normal duties within four longer days instead of the conventional five, has moved from a fringe experiment to a serious policy proposal, with the Portuguese government, together with a number of British firms, having completed trials between 2022 and 2024. The rationale behind the proposal rests on a simple observation, namely that the hours spent at a desk and the work actually completed are two different quantities, and that the second does not grow in proportion to the first. Shorter weeks, in the words of its supporters, function as an instrument for raising productivity and retaining staff, while critics warn that the same arrangement may compress more pressure into fewer days, particularly in the sectors where the work must be delivered in person. This essay, therefore, will examine both the benefits and drawbacks of the four-day week, and argue that its value depends on the design of the trial rather than on the principle itself.

The premier advantage of a four-day week lies in the productivity gains which follow from a shorter and better organised working day. The largest trial in the United Kingdom, coordinated by the research group Autonomy, covered sixty-one companies and reported that revenue stayed broadly the same or improved in most of the participating firms, with staff turnover falling sharply and sick leave reduced by roughly two thirds (Autonomy, 2023). Similar results were recorded by the New Zealand insurer Perpetual Guardian, where the shift to four days was accompanied by a measurable rise in output per worker, attributed by the management to shorter meetings and clearer priorities (Barnes, 2020). Such evidence suggests that a substantial share of the working week is consumed by activities which add little value, including unnecessary meetings, repeated emails and informal interruptions, and that the removal of these costs can fund the lost day without any reduction in output.

Besides the improvement in output, a shorter week also produces benefits which fall outside the balance sheet. Employees who gain a weekday report better sleep, more time with their families and a reduction in the feelings of exhaustion which accumulate across the week, and these improvements appear in the health measures collected during the British trial, together with the self-reports gathered by the Icelandic experiments between 2015 and 2019 (Haraldsson and Kellam, 2021). The recruitment effect should not be underestimated either. Firms which offer four days receive more applications per vacancy, and the retention of experienced staff carries a value which rarely appears in the accounts, particularly in the professions where the training of a replacement requires years.

The picture is less favourable when the working time is examined from the side of the customer. In the sectors where the service must be delivered in person, including hospitals, schools, transport and retail, the shortening of the week does not reduce the hours which the organisation must cover, and the same coverage must therefore be provided by more staff or by longer shifts. The British trials concentrated disproportionately on the office-based firms, whose output does not depend on the physical presence of the worker at a particular hour, with the result that these results are difficult to transfer to the sectors which employ a larger share of the workforce. An arrangement which improves the lives of software engineers may leave a nurse with the same number of hours compressed into fewer and longer days, and the fatigue which follows from a twelve-hour shift is not obviously an improvement over the conventional pattern.

Another drawback of the four-day week is the intensification of work which accompanies the compression of the same output into less time. Employees in the trials report that the shorter week is not experienced as a gift of free time but as a demand for greater concentration, with the informal breaks removed and the pace of the working day increased, and the arrangement may therefore function as a transfer of the productivity gain from the worker to the employer. The same logic applies to the boundaries of the working day. Where the volume of work is fixed by the client rather than by the firm, as in the legal and consulting professions, the reduction of the available days is frequently followed by the extension of the working evening, and the total hours may remain unchanged while the formal week is described as shorter.

To conclude, a four-day week is not only an effective instrument for raising productivity in the office-based sectors, but employees can also gain improvements in health and family life which carry a value beyond the wage. Nevertheless, the arrangement can be deceptive in effect, since the same reduction of the formal week may compress the same work into fewer and longer days, and the sectors which require continuous coverage are rarely able to adopt it on the same terms. Despite these concerns, the four-day week should still be tested rather than rejected, with the trials designed to measure output and health together, and with the coverage requirements of the front-line services treated as a separate problem, so that the shorter week is delivered as a genuine reduction of work rather than as a compression of the same burden.
