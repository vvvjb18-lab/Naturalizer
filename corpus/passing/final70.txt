One of my goals for better management in the academic life is to eradicate procrastination.
As something that has been with me since I was in primary school, the habit of 
procrastination can cause severe psychological pain, acute anxiety, and a dip in academic 
performance due to the delay of academic related activities (Sirois & Pychyl, 2013; Tice & 
Baumeister, 1997). Hence, it is extremely important for me to tackle the procrastination issue 
and establish a disciplined and proactive study schedule in order to grow as a person.
As a precursor to formulating goals to combat my procrastination, I would like to share my 
personal experiences and bad study habits to get things started. Firstly, during my primary 
school years, my parents were very demanding of my academic performance and kept a very 
close eye on my homework. If I made small mistakes or didn’t get full marks in the tests, they
would discipline me severely. It would make me feel really scared and stressed out. Due to all
the huge pressure from my family, I started procrastinating on my homework and revising 
until the last minute so that I wouldn’t need to be anxious about doing things imperfectly. 
Thinking back to my childhood, I realise my parents’ parenting style was authoritarian which 
made me a chronic procrastinator. Authoritarian parents are very demanding and controlling. 
This often leads to children being apprehensive and susceptible to stress and afraid of failure. 
(Baumrind, 1971). Moreover, Wood (2013) states that our self-concept is highly influenced 
by reflected appraisal, which is how we believe that important others perceive and evaluate 
us. Through internalising my parents’ evaluations, I associated my worth with getting perfect 
grades. Procrastinating became a self-preservation tactic to maintain my self-esteem. By 
procrastinating, I can always attribute any disappointing outcome to lack of time rather than 
lack of ability. Over time, this thinking further strengthened my external locus of control. 
(Hanna et al., 2010).

Rather than making ambiguous claims, I created a collection of self-monitoring aids 
(following "SMART" guidelines) to develop solid study habits and to avoid the last-minute 
cramming for the next month.
'S' (specific): I will divide each upcoming assignments into three sub-tasks; analysing and 
brainstorming ideas to make an outline for questions; making a rough draft; doing final 
proofreading. With this focus approach, I can prevent myself from being overloaded by 
massive academic tasks. 'M' (measurable): I will allocate six hours for exam revision per 
week. It means I will do two hours revision from 7-9pm on every Tuesday, Thursday, 
Saturday. I will monitor my study progress by using Yeolpumta app which keeps track of 
how long I study for and the topics I covered. And for the better understanding, I will work 
on a pile of past papers and make a record for my accuracy rate. If the accuracy rate is lower 
than 70%, I will put more efforts to review my weak topics in the next week. 'A' (achievable):
I will set a quiet space for studying at home for my consistent revision time. I can keep 
myself off digital distractions through the app-blocking function in Yeolpumta app. In case of
unavoidable event, I will make sure to reschedule it in next evening to keep up my weekly 
commitment for being consistent. 'R' (relevant): by setting up my structured study plan, I can 
enhance my internal locus of control and develop a positive self-concept. This indicates that 
the academic achievement is affected by consistent personal efforts. (Hanna et al., 2010) 'T' 
(time-bound): I will consistently keep up the structured plan for one month. Also, I will check
my progress on a weekly basis to ensure that I can build study habits sustainably instead of 
being caught in last-minute cramming.
