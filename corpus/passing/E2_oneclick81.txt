The Case for Taxing Sugar-Sweetened Beverages Taxing sugary drinks is a good idea.
Obesity and the diet related disease is one of the most grave public health problems of the
twenty-first century. Of the myriad of factors which cause obesity, the excessive 
consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages plays an extremely significant role. Sugar-
sweetened beverages contain massive amount of free sugars, yet fail to induce the sense 
of fullness. Therefore, governments should levy a tax on the sugar-sweetened beverage. 
The reasons are that a sugar-sweetened beverage tax can lead to a reduction in 
consumption, bring money into the government for public health programs, and change 
the corporate behaviour to encourage the development of healthier products.
Firstly, there is empirical evidence to show that sugary drinks' consumption is 
successfully reduced through taxation. For example, in Mexico, after the introduction of a
one penny per ounce tax there was an average two-year reduction of 7.6 percent in 
consumption of the taxed drinks, which was most prevalent in low-income households 
(Colchero et al., 2016). Additionally, studies into hypothetical UK price policy show that 
a twenty percent tax would lead to a reduction in the prevalence of obesity by 1.3 percent 
in adults, thus preventing 180,000 cases (Mytton et al., 2012). This shows that it is 
possible to effectively direct consumers away from harmful products through price 
signals.
Secondly, the tax revenue provides dedicated funds for health promotion. In the first US 
city to implement a soda tax, Berkeley, California, tax revenue has been directed towards 
funding school nutrition programmes and community health activities, amplifying the 
total impact of the policy (Falbe et al., 2016). As sugary drink consumption is particularly
high among deprived populations, the ability to direct dedicated tax revenue towards 
these most affected groups means that diet-related disease health impacts and equity 
goals can be addressed at the same time.
Sugar taxes are regressive and paternalistic, hitting the poor hardest in terms of cost. 
While there is a truth to this criticism, two factors are not taken into account: Firstly, the 
health impact of sugary drinks is also regressive (i.e. worse for the poor), and secondly, 
progressive use of tax revenue can easily outweigh the extra cost. Also, the industrial 
reaction to taxations indicates that not only paternalism is at play: with the UK tax, more 
than half of products got reformulated to lower their sugar content (for the benefit of all 
consumers, even if purchasing behaviour is not altered (Bandy et al., 2020)).
To conclude, taxing sugary drinks is a proportionate and evidence-based intervention to a
serious public health issue. It curbs consumption in the face of failed voluntary measures,
finances supporting health interventions and encourages reformulation for health. 
Hesitant governments must realise that it is no longer a case of whether sugary drink 
taxes work, but how to make them fair.
