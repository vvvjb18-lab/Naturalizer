Body Paragraph 1 with 1 Source 
Topic Sentence:(1 Complete Sentence) 
First, caffeine keeps students awake by masking the brain’s sleepiness signals, and this alertness is 
dose-dependent: moderate doses raise it, while high doses are linked to nervousness and insomnia. 
Supporting Ideas: (3 Bullet Points) 
• Caffeine’s most recognised action is to compete with adenosine at receptors in the central 
nervous system, blocking the signals that normally produce tiredness. 
• This blockade works even at the low doses in a single cup of coffee and releases dopamine, 
noradrenalin and glutamate, so moderate doses measurably raise arousal and alertness. 
• Very high doses produce the opposite: nervousness, insomnia and an exaggerated, restless 
alertness.

Topic Sentence:(1 Complete Sentence) 
Second, direct measurement of a single dose in adolescent students shows that caffeine slows the 
decline in attention during a long task. 
Supporting Ideas: (3 Bullet Points) 
• Double-blind, placebo-controlled investigation of caffeine effects on sustained attention in 31 
school children, 12-17 years of age, during a 33-min performance of sustained attention after 
0, 1 or 3 mg caffeine/kg body weight. 
• Over time accuracy tended to decline, but this decay was reduced by caffeine; overall 
caffeine was significantly better than placebo (p = .003) and the high dosage prevented loss 
of performance sooner. 
• The improvement was observed at the level of the task, rather than self-reported sense of 
wakefulness. While the participants were light everyday users (median 28mg/day), the effect 
is unlikely to have been mediated by alleviation of withdrawal symptoms, however due to the 
nature of the design (i.e. adolescent, acute dose) the generalisability to university study is 
constrained.

Finally, higher caffeine consumption per day was related to lower global sleep quality in 
undergraduates. 
Supporting Ideas: (3 Bullet Points) 
• Caffeine consumed from different sources and sleep in 886 undergraduates: number of 
milligrams per day of caffeine consumed from coffee, tea, chocolate, cola and energy drinks 
was reported along with sleep measured by a validated six-item questionnaire. 
• Sleep quality, as indicated by higher scores reflecting worse sleep, deteriorated slightly for 
every extra milligram of caffeine taken daily (β = .194, p < .001), thus the strongest predictor 
(after accounting for lifestyle factors). 
• Based on self-reported cross sectional data from a single university it's an association but not 
evidence of a cause-effect relationship in which the only factor involved is caffeine on poor 
sleep.
